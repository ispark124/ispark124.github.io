community & commodity

Isaiah Park

we've commodified community. we've bundled it up, marketed it, and made a killing off of it. i've been saying it for months (i was not the first nor will i be the last), but the next stage in consumerism is a market of connection—of selling "community"—and it's already happening. pickleball club this, running club that, "come with me to an underground bar to play mahjong"—social clubs are the hottest product and community will continue to be sold as such. not only has this been prevalent throughout major cities and social media, but it's slowly crept into the way we view social gatherings as a whole. every gathering, every party needs to be marketed, sent out on a partiful, and posted about on social media. community is being sold everywhere.

let me back up a bit. for a while now, everyone has been talking about the loneliness epidemic plaguing our society these days, and we are not meeting people organically anymore. i definitely don't need to beat the dead horse of what social media and being chronically online has done to us as people, or that attendance in traditional, institutional places of gathering (church, community centers, public libraries, etc) have taken a sharp decline. the bottom line is people are craving real, intimate connection. there is a void that's been created when the spaces where people used to consistently gather are gone and they are instead filled with a fake, flashy version of it online. i'd love to get into a tangent about how we have relegated our need for community, identity, and meaning to the workplace, but that's a whole other topic for another time. all of this to say that we have a society that is the loneliest it's ever been.

working in tandem with our loneliness epidemic, we have the "entrepreneurialization" of our society. what do i mean by this? we have been sold this idea (yes, sold at a cost) that anyone can become an entrepreneur, an influencer, and not only anyone can but also everyone should. in the society we live in, it's how we're supposed to take ownership of our time and our lives. we've subscribed to the notion that we are somehow overturning the status quo and paving our own way when we become an influencer/entrepreneur and escape the traditional 9 to 5. we've believed the idea that true freedom is becoming the boss (god) of our own lives. in doing so, we've unknowingly aligned ourselves with the motives and desires of the top CEOs and trillionaires of the world.

under this entrepreneurial framework, we see them not as anomalies or exploitative, but as the shining examples of those who have entrepreneur-ed their way to the top. sure, we can look down with distaste on the decisions they've made, but the cynical motivator is that if we play by the same rules we can one day make it too. as if in order to grow we have to take part in what growth looks like to the world. we sell ourselves to the capitalistic scheme and call it becoming our own boss. as long as this is the mindset, we proliferate the desires of the obscenely rich, the "successful," the Algorithm, and pass it off as our own. the desire for our entire life to be solely for profit and capital accumulation. and to be clear, i don't have a problem with money, but i do have a problem with misaligned success. we're barreling towards a future whose values we don't believe in.

to further expound upon the idea of misaligned success, i found myself asking, "in the entrepreneurialization of our generation, what is the metric by which people measure success?" in this new influencer world, we play by the Algorithm's rules for engagement, virality, and follower-ship, which do not actually reflect true engagement, virality, or follower-ship, but are rooted in predation and exploitation. true growth, in the ways that humanity is meant to flourish, is not determined by an algorithm that feeds off engagement farming, escaping reality, and carnality. true growth is found by seeking what is Good, Beautiful, or True, and not tawdry imitations of its fruit for our attention. and so, our influencers, our entrepreneurs, are playing by the rules of this predatory, hegemonic frame.

so with the Algorithm that works to promote virality, AI slop that makes anything possible, and a vulnerable, lonely generation to be caught up in it, the barrier for entry to become an entrepreneur/influencer has never been lower.

it is here we find the rise of social clubs and the state of community as we know them: a lonely generation meets hungry influencers and entrepreneurs operating under a framework of virality, greed, and "good intentions." so it is not that i inherently have a problem with social clubs, but we have to understand what is actually being sold and what is being bought and call it what it has become: a commodity.

when community becomes a commodity, it is no longer operating with the currency of Love and Trust, but rather of pleasure and entertainment. people are entering these spaces looking to take instead of looking to give, because that is exactly what has been marketed to them. when community becomes a commodity, it is just another good to be bought and sold. this means that the moment community becomes an inconvenience to us or no longer pleasurable, we can drop it and buy the next one, the newer one, the flashier one.

in many ways, that is exactly why these places feel so hollow. what these influencers and entrepreneurs are selling is a caricature of what a community is supposed to be. it's measured in attendance, influencer presence, and social media hype rather than the immeasurable love and affection for one another. in all of this, we've created an abundance of third spaces that are devoid of the genuine connections that make them worth filling. we've simply created more holes to fill. how ironic.

i came across this quote the other day that acutely articulates what i've been feeling. dietrich bonhoeffer said, "he who loves his dream of community more than the christian community itself becomes a destroyer of the latter." we've clung to this dream of community because that's what's easy, what's marketable, what gets pushed by the Algorithm. but by losing sight of the reality of Love and the inconvenient, uncomfortable work it is to truly take part in it, we've cheapened community to a gathering of like-minded individuals that want to be pleased and entertained (don't even get me started on the state of the church today).

and so what is the way forward? how do we get out of this?

we've tried doing the taking, the deification of self, the consumerist mindset, and found that a community filled with that is actually hollow. how quick are we to leave a conversation at a party or drop a "friend" the moment it actually costs us something, whether that be our time, our emotional capacity, or even our social clout? multiply that by however many people in a room and we very quickly find ourselves in the situation we're in today.

i can't say i know for sure, but i do know the way forward has to look radically different from the world we're living in and the framework we're operating under. if we want to leave the enslavement of a consumerist community we must actually become enslaved to another kind of community: one of radical Love, Generosity, and Trust. we enter into spaces not looking to take but instead to give, not looking to serve self but serve others. we know that there is not a lack of desire to know people (we're all going to these community building events for a reason!), but the problem actually lies with the lack of desire to venture beyond pleasantries and into where costly heart work is, to give of self. to fill a community with something genuine, we must be willing to be inconvenienced for the sake of one another. in a phrase, we must love one another the way Christ has loved us.

i heard a story about my friend that moved me to tears. they never would share this, but i truly believe it is the heart of God: the other day my friend was busy and couldn't go to a friendsgiving party. when their event was finished, they showed up to the friendsgiving party hours later when everything was already over and wrapped up. they didn't come to hang out, they didn't even ask anyone to save a plate for them, but instead they went to the kitchen sink and they washed the dishes.

they came to wash the dishes.

in a world so focused on self, so focused on profit, so focused on taking, this holy act is unthinkable. it was a decision to lay aside all sense of self and to give.

"greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down their life for their friends." john 15:13

i don't think we need to stop with the social clubs. i don't think we need to stop with the partifuls and possibly even the social media pushing. i do think we need to take a hard look at the way that consumerism, pleasure, and entertainment have infested our view of community and relationships.

community was never supposed to be an event to be sold. i'm reflecting on the best times i’ve had with my new friends in austin and they have been in beautifully ordinary, selfless moments. they've been around a dining table in someone’s home after cooking and cleaning. they've been on a park bench eating ice cream together, sharing about what we've been grieving and offering each other our hearts. they've been in cars giving each other rides and spending long drives talking. every day is an invitation into the extraordinary ordinary moments when we can enter into community with those around us.

i pray that in this holy pursuit of community, we would find Christ. may we all be moved to live like Christ, to die like Christ, and to love one another.
